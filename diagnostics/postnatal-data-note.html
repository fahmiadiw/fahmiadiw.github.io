Where counts are genuinely independent, 1.5–7.7% of mothers are lost between the first and final postnatal visit (median 2.6%).

How strong is each signal?

Strong · zero drop-out over 42 days

7 districts

KF lengkap equals or almost equals KF1: every mother seen once was supposedly seen at every later visit. Lampung Tengah, Pesawaran, Mesuji, Tulang Bawang Barat and Metro lose none; Lampung Timur and Pringsewu lose fewer than 0.5%.

Most likely: KF lengkap is not counted on its own but copied from KF1.

Medium · delivery = first postnatal visit

6 districts

Possible if every facility birth gets KF1 before discharge and no home birth ever does, but an exact match in districts with thousands of births is unlikely.

Most likely: facility births used as a stand-in for KF1.

Weak · KF1 = vitamin A

10 districts

Vitamin A is often given during the first postnatal visit, so equal counts can be genuine. On its own this proves nothing; it only matters next to the other two signals.

Most likely: real, or a form default.

Bandar Lampung: #1 on antenatal care, but unverified
  • K6 (19,631) is exactly equal to K4: every woman with four visits supposedly reached six, including two doctor visits. Across the province, coverage drops from 93.8% to 56.7% at this step.
  • First visits (100.7%) and facility births (100.7%) exceed the target, so the estimated number of pregnancies is too low, probably because women from nearby districts use city facilities.
  • Good access to private obstetricians can explain a small K4–K6 gap, but not an exact match.
What this means for planning
  • The provincial figure of 91.7% complete postnatal care rests heavily on counts that were probably not collected separately. The real figure is very likely lower; this data cannot say by how much.
  • Pesawaran looks like it has no postnatal follow-up problem at all. That is exactly where a program should check first, because a drop-out there would be invisible.
  • This is not evidence of fraud. Form defaults, aggregate reporting from health centres, and treating postnatal care as one package are all more plausible causes.

How to confirm it

  1. Go one level down. Request health-centre-level maternal and child health monitoring reports (PWS KIA) for the flagged districts; copying usually shows up in specific facilities.
  2. Check against registers. Sample postnatal visit registers in 2–3 health centres and compare them with the reported counts.
  3. Look across years. Compare 2021 and 2023 to see whether zero drop-out is new or a long-standing reporting habit.
  4. Fix the form. Add the rules above at entry, so a repeated value triggers a prompt instead of reaching the dashboard.
